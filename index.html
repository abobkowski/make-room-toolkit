The problem

Conference stages remain staff-dominated spaces. SaP literature offers rich curriculum frameworks, but rarely extends to public scholarly dissemination — leaving student voice absent or tokenised at conferences.

The approach

Whole-class partnership routines (co-creating agendas, norms, and outcomes) build an inclusive co-creation mindset across the whole cohort — not just "super-engaged" students — then extend via transparent opt-in invitations.

The evidence

Enhanced confidence in professional communication, improved collaboration, and stronger scholarly identity for students (Weller et al., 2013; Giuliano et al., 2022). Richer SoTL insights for educators (Barrie, 2006).

How to use this

Navigate through the six components using the tabs above. Each tool is a practical, ready-to-adapt resource. Start with Guiding principles, then work through invitation → roles → slides → safety → recognition → debrief.

Guiding principles

These five principles underpin all six toolkit components. They draw on Brookfield's (2017) four lenses, refined through participant feedback from classroom and conference settings across multiple institutions.

01
Equity before invitation
Whole-class routines must build partnership norms before any individual invitation is extended. The invite only works when co-creation is already normalised in the learning environment.
02
Transparent opt-in
Invitations are open, honest, and low-pressure. Students understand what is involved, what recognition they will receive, and that participation is genuinely optional — including former students.
03
Shared authorship
Student co-presenters are not helpers or props. They co-design content, co-own ideas, and are credited equally. The slide workflow and role menu operationalise this principle.
04
Safety is non-negotiable
Psychological and practical safety checks are built into preparation, not added as afterthoughts. Students set their own comfort boundaries; the debrief ensures ongoing safety after the event.
05
Recognition matters
Student contributions deserve formal acknowledgement beyond a thank-you. The recognition tier system ensures students gain tangible benefit — CV credit, referees, formal citations — appropriate to their contribution.
Methodological basis

This toolkit was developed using Brookfield's (2017) four lenses — educator reflection, student reflection, peer/colleague feedback, and literature — refined through a fifth lens: direct participant feedback from classroom and conference deployments at the University of Sydney, MOBTS Salem, MOBTS-Oceania Auckland, Sydney Teaching Symposium, and the Global SaP Roundtable (ITLI).

01

Invitation script

Transparent, opt-in language for inviting students — including former students after course completion — to co-present. The script is a template: adapt the bracketed sections to your context, conference, and relationship with the student.

Email / message template

"Hi [Name], I'm presenting at [conference/event] on [date] on [topic area]. Given your work in [specific area or project], I'd love to invite you to co-present with me if that's something you'd be interested in exploring."

"This is a genuine invitation — completely optional — and we'd design the session together from the start. There's no expectation to take on more than you're comfortable with, and I'd make sure you receive [recognition: formal acknowledgement / co-authorship / CV reference / other]."

"Would you be open to a short conversation to hear more about what it might involve? No pressure either way."

Note: Send this as a direct personal message, not to a group. Use the student's name and reference something specific to their work to signal genuine interest, not a blanket invitation.

What makes an effective invitation?

P
Personal and specificReference the student's actual contribution or interest. Generic invitations signal tokenism.
O
Opt-in, not opt-outMake refusal easy and consequence-free. Power dynamics mean students may feel obliged to say yes.
R
Recognition upfrontState what credit or acknowledgement the student will receive before they agree. Remove ambiguity.
T
Time and scope clearBe transparent about time commitment, role scope, and what co-design will involve from the outset.

Former students: Inviting alumni preserves the value of the partnership beyond the course. Use similar language, adding context about what has changed in your thinking since their graduation. Alumni often bring a unique dual perspective — student and emerging professional — that enriches conference dialogue.

02

Role menu

A clear menu of flexible roles allows students to contribute in ways that match their skills, confidence, and career goals. Roles are not ranked — each is equally valued. Students may select a single role or combine elements across roles.

Presenting
Co-presenter
Takes the stage to deliver content, answer questions, and represent the research alongside the academic partner.
Co-designs and presents 1+ slides or section
Participates in Q&A dialogue
Shares personal perspective or experience
Facilitating
Co-facilitator
Facilitates discussion or audience interaction before, during, or after the presentation without necessarily presenting content.
Designs and runs audience activities or polls
Manages questions from the floor
Guides small-group breakout discussion
Designing
Content co-designer
Works with the academic partner to co-design the content, narrative, and slides — may or may not present on the day.
Co-writes key sections of the presentation
Designs visual or narrative elements
Provides critical feedback on drafts
Provocation
Student provocateur
Opens or closes the session with a brief student provocation — a short, distinctive statement of student perspective or challenge to the audience.
Prepares a 3–5 minute provocation or reflection
Offers a student lens on the research question
May be pre-recorded if preferred
Research
Research collaborator
Contributes to the underlying research or scholarship being presented — co-author, data contributor, or conceptual partner.
Named on any outputs or publications
Contributed to data, analysis, or writing
Academic authorship protocols apply
Support
Event supporter
Provides practical support before or on the day — a lighter entry point for students new to conference contexts who want to be involved without presenting.
Manages tech, timer, or logistics on the day
Observes and provides feedback post-event
Pathway to co-presenting at future events

Tip: Share this role menu with students during the invitation conversation — not after they've agreed. Seeing the full range of options allows students to self-select based on comfort and capacity, and prevents the default assumption that co-presenting means delivering the whole talk.

03

Shared slide workflow

A structured process to ensure slides are genuinely co-designed — not handed to a student to "add something to." Equal authorship produces better presentations and embeds real partnership into the artefact.

1
Separate brief conversation before opening a deck

Before any slide is created, agree on: the key argument, who the audience is, what each person is most excited to say, and what the student's perspective uniquely contributes. 20 minutes here saves hours later.

Both
2
Draft a shared narrative outline (not slides)

Map the session arc together — in words, not slides. A shared document with headings and 2–3 sentences per section. Each partner identifies which sections they feel ownership of.

Both
3
Student drafts their section(s) first

Give the student the first draft of their slides, unprompted by the academic. Avoids the student reproducing the academic's framing. Even rough early drafts are better than polished slides handed down for comment.

Student
4
Academic drafts their section(s)

Parallel drafting — each person owns their slides. At this stage, avoid reviewing each other's drafts; maintain independent voice before the merge.

Academic
5
Joint review and integration session

Review both sets of slides together. Look for: narrative flow, duplication, gaps, and — critically — where each voice is clearly present. Adjust for coherence without homogenising distinct perspectives.

Both
6
Practice Q&A together

Run a mock Q&A session. Agree in advance on how you'll handle questions: who responds first, what to do if one person doesn't know the answer, how to support each other mid-question. This is critical for student confidence.

Both
7
Final run-through with safety check

Before the event: confirm the student is still comfortable, no content has changed without their input, and they know the practical logistics (room, tech, timing, who to contact if something goes wrong).

05

Recognition guidance

Student contributions deserve formal, tangible acknowledgement — not just gratitude. This tier system matches recognition to the nature and depth of the student's contribution.

Tier 1
Acknowledgement Minimum — applies to all contributions
Named in presentation slides
Named in any published abstract or proceedings
Verbal acknowledgement to audience
LinkedIn recommendation / endorsement offered
Written thank-you and debrief from academic partner
Tier 2
Professional credential For active presenting or facilitating roles
Formal academic reference letter
Letter confirming role for CV / scholarship applications
Micro-credential or institutional record (where available)
Named co-presenter in conference program
Invitation to future events as named partner
Tier 3
Research co-authorship Where substantial intellectual contribution has been made
Named co-author on any written output or publication
CRediT author contribution statement
Formal co-investigator or research partner status
Paid or stipended partnership (where institutional mechanisms exist)
Pathway into Honours, research assistant, or SoTL co-investigator role

Widening access to recognition

Current SaP literature notes that recognition too often defaults to informal thanks — which disadvantages students who most need tangible credentials (first-in-family students, international students, those without existing professional networks). Making Tier 1 recognition the minimum, non-negotiable floor ensures partnership is genuinely beneficial to all students who participate.

Discuss and agree on recognition tier before the student commits. Do not adjust recognition downwards after the fact. If institutional mechanisms for higher tiers don't exist at your institution, name that gap to the student honestly and advocate to create them.

06

Debrief template

A structured post-event reflection for both partners. Debrief within one week of the event. Responses can be used as data for Brookfield's fourth and fifth lenses. Type your notes here and print or copy to save.

Student partner reflection

What felt most comfortable about the co-presenting experience?

What felt most challenging or uncomfortable — and why?

What graduate qualities or skills did you develop or demonstrate?

What would you change if co-presenting again?

Was the recognition you received appropriate and meaningful?

Academic partner reflection

What did the student's perspective contribute that you couldn't have offered alone?

Where did the preparation process work well — or break down?

What audience reactions or questions were shaped by the student's presence?

What will you do differently in the next invitation or co-design process?

What SoTL or teaching insights did this experience generate for you?

Joint reflection (both partners)

What worked well in how we divided and shared responsibility?

Did the power dynamic show up in ways we hadn't anticipated?

Would we do this again — and what would need to be different?

Next steps

Based on this debrief, what are your commitments for the next co-presenting opportunity?

Who else in your department or network should you share this experience with?

Data use note: These reflections can contribute to Brookfield's (2017) first and second lenses (educator and student reflection) and the toolkit's fifth lens (participant feedback). With informed consent, debrief notes may be used as data for scholarship of teaching and learning (SoTL) enquiry. Always comply with your institutional ethics requirements — including HREC approval — before collecting, retaining, or publishing debrief responses.
